# KelpDAO rsETH — nobody stole a signing key; the verifier signed honestly over a chain it was being lied to about — KelpDAO rsETH / LayerZero OFT / Unichain → Ethereum — 2026-04-18

**Loss:** **116,500 rsETH, ~\$292M**, released from the Ethereum-side contract against a **burn on Unichain that never happened**. A follow-up attempt targeting a further **~\$95M** did not succeed. The damage did not stop at Kelp: the attacker **deposited unbacked rsETH into Aave V3 as collateral and borrowed against it**, leaving Aave facing **up to ~\$230.1M in bad debt** on the worst-case scenario (**~\$124M** if the shortfall were spread across all rsETH holders as a ~15% depeg; **~\$230M** if isolated to L2s, concentrated on **Arbitrum and Mantle**). By **19:00 UTC on 2026-04-18** all rsETH and wrsETH reserves across Aave V3 deployments were **frozen, LTV set to zero**, and interest-rate models adjusted. A cross-protocol coalition — **"DeFi United"**, with Aave, Lido, Ether.fi, Ethena, Mantle and the Aave DAO — raised **~\$160M** of the ~\$200M required. KelpDAO subsequently **migrated rsETH to Chainlink CCIP**.

**OAK Techniques observed:** **OAK-T10.002.001** (Off-chain Observer Source-Event Forgery — *primary, confirmed by LayerZero Labs' own incident report and by independent forensic analysis*. The **LayerZero Labs DVN** — the off-chain verifier whose attestation released the funds — **parsed correctly, reasoned correctly and signed honestly.** What it read was false: the **RPC nodes supplying its view of the source chain had been poisoned**, and reported a **burn event on Unichain that never occurred**. See [`techniques/T10.002.001-off-chain-observer-source-event-forgery.md`](../techniques/T10.002.001-off-chain-observer-source-event-forgery.md)). **OAK-T15.001** (Social Engineering of Operator Personnel — the entry vector on **2026-03-06**: a **LayerZero Labs developer** was socially engineered, **six weeks before** the funds moved). **OAK-T15.004** (Operator Credential Compromise — **session keys** harvested from that developer, used to pivot into LayerZero's **RPC cloud environment**). **OAK-T14.003** (Restaking Cascading Risk — the Aave leg, with the mechanism corrected below: the cascade was driven by the **attacker depositing unbacked rsETH as collateral**, not only by existing positions falling underwater).

**Explicitly not OAK-T10.001** (Validator / Signer-Key Compromise). **No cryptographic or signing key was stolen.** This is the load-bearing correction in this file, and it inverts the mitigation: the DVN's signature over the fraudulent message was **cryptographically valid and produced by the legitimate key**, because the key was never the target. Key hygiene, HSMs, signer rotation and threshold-signature hardening would each have left this attack completely unaffected. See *Discussion* for the correction record.

**Attribution:** **confirmed** — **DPRK threat actor TraderTraitor, also tracked as UNC4899** ([`OAK-G01`](../actors/OAK-G01-lazarus.md)), attributed independently by **Mandiant**, **CrowdStrike** and **zeroShadow**, and named as such in **LayerZero Labs' own published incident report**.

**Key teaching point:** **A verifier is only as truthful as its view of the chain, and almost nobody treats "where does my verifier get its data?" as part of the trust model.** Every control in this bridge's design pointed at the signature: who holds the key, how it is stored, how many must agree. The attacker never went near it. They went one layer below — to the **RPC nodes the verifier queries** — and changed what reality looked like from where the verifier was standing. Three details make the operation worth studying as a template rather than a one-off. First, the poisoned nodes were **memory-patched to keep telling the truth to LayerZero's own monitoring** while lying to the DVN, so the deception was **targeted at exactly one consumer** and internal dashboards stayed green. Second, the attacker **DoS'd an external RPC provider** to force DVN signing onto the two nodes they controlled — the redundancy that existed was *removed on demand*, which means redundancy you do not verify under adversarial conditions is not redundancy. Third, none of it would have paid out under a **multi-DVN configuration**: rsETH ran a **single verifier**, and that single choice is what separated a contained intrusion from \$292M. The controls follow directly: **quorum across independently operated verifiers**, **source diversity and integrity attestation for RPC data**, **custody reconciliation before release** (a burn with no matching custody change is detectable at payment one), and **treating the data path into a verifier as in-scope for the same review tier as the key path.**

## Summary

**KelpDAO** issues **rsETH**, a liquid restaking token, and deployed it across chains using **LayerZero's OFT** standard. Cross-chain transfers are authorised by a **DVN** (Decentralized Verifier Network) — an off-chain verifier that observes the source chain and attests that the source-side event happened, on the strength of which the destination-side contract acts.

rsETH's OApp was configured with a **single verifier: the LayerZero Labs DVN.** No second DVN had to agree before funds moved.

The intrusion began on **2026-03-06**, six weeks before anything was stolen. An attacker **socially engineered a LayerZero Labs developer**, harvested **session keys**, and used them to pivot into **LayerZero's RPC cloud environment**. There they obtained **the list of RPC endpoints the DVN was querying**, gained access to **two independent internal nodes running on separate clusters**, and replaced the software on them — **patching node memory so that LayerZero's own tooling continued to receive correct responses**, while the responses served to the DVN were manipulated.

To close the last gap, the attacker ran a **denial-of-service attack against an external RPC provider**, so that DVN signing could draw only on the two internal nodes they had already compromised.

On **2026-04-18**, the poisoned nodes reported a **burn of rsETH on Unichain**. It had not happened. The DVN — reading only the nodes it could reach, all of which agreed — produced a **valid attestation** for the message. The Ethereum-side contract honoured it and **released 116,500 rsETH, about \$292M**. A follow-up attempt against a further **~\$95M** failed.

The attacker then **supplied the unbacked rsETH to Aave V3 as collateral and borrowed against it**, converting a Kelp loss into a system-wide one. Aave froze all rsETH and wrsETH reserves by **19:00 UTC the same day**, set LTV to zero and adjusted interest-rate models, facing **up to ~\$230.1M** in bad debt. The **DeFi United** coalition raised **~\$160M** toward it.

**Mandiant, CrowdStrike and zeroShadow** attributed the operation to **DPRK's TraderTraitor / UNC4899**. LayerZero Labs published a detailed incident report, made **multiple DVN attestors a baseline requirement**, and **rebuilt the compromised cloud infrastructure** with hardened baselines and **just-in-time privilege elevation**. KelpDAO moved rsETH to **Chainlink CCIP**.

## What defenders observed

- **The gap between intrusion and theft was six weeks, and every day of it was inside someone else's infrastructure.** Initial access on 2026-03-06, funds on 2026-04-18. This is not a smash-and-grab against a contract; it is a **patient infrastructure operation with a bridge as the payout**, which is the DPRK bridge-targeting pattern the corpus records elsewhere. The detection opportunity was not on 04-18 — it was in the **six weeks of anomalous access to an RPC cloud environment**.
- **Selective lying is what defeated monitoring, and it should be assumed by default now.** The nodes returned **correct data to LayerZero's own tools** and **false data to the DVN**. Any assurance derived from "our dashboards looked normal" is worthless against an attacker who can choose per-consumer responses. **Verification must be performed by the consumer that acts on the data**, not by a sibling system reading the same nodes.
- **The attacker removed the redundancy rather than defeating it.** There was an external RPC provider. It was DoS'd. Redundancy that can be **reduced by an attacker at the moment of use** is a liveness feature, not a security control — and the correct test is whether the system **fails closed** when its sources shrink to a subset. Here, shrinking the source set to two agreeing nodes was treated as consensus.
- **Single-verifier configuration is the whole difference between contained and catastrophic.** LayerZero's report is explicit that the blast radius was bounded to rsETH **as a direct consequence of the OApp's single-DVN setup**, with no contagion to other assets or applications. The same intrusion against a 2-of-3 configuration produces a **failed attestation and an alert**, not a payout. The choice was made at configuration time, by an integrator, long before any attacker existed.
- **A custody reconciliation catches this at payment one, as it does for every other anchor in this class.** Release 116,500 rsETH only if the locked/issued invariant moved by 116,500. The invariant broke on the **first** release and nothing was reading it.
- **The contagion path ran through the attacker's own deposit, not through price alone.** The attacker took the unbacked rsETH **into Aave V3 as collateral** and borrowed. That converts a bridge failure into a lending-market failure **immediately and deliberately**, without waiting for a depeg to propagate. Listing an asset whose supply integrity depends on a third party's off-chain verifier means **inheriting that verifier's failure modes onto your own balance sheet** — which is the argument for supply-attested collateral (proof of backing) rather than price-attested collateral.

## Discussion

### Correction record (2026-09-16)

**This file previously classified the incident as `OAK-T10.001` (Validator / Signer-Key Compromise), described the mechanism as "a compromise of LayerZero's messaging infrastructure, which functionally serves as the signer," and recorded the attribution as `pseudonymous`. All three were wrong, and are corrected above.** Per `CORRECTIONS.md`, the superseded claim is recorded here rather than silently removed.

The provenance is worth stating plainly, because it is a repeat of a known failure mode in this corpus. The original text was written in the **v0.1 bulk release** (commit `f32645a`), while the LayerZero investigation was still open — the file said so itself, listing "key-theft, insider action, or protocol-level vulnerability" as open possibilities and inviting a future update. Its **Public references section contained no URLs at all**, only generic descriptions of documents that would presumably exist ("Kelp DAO official incident announcement and post-mortem", "On-chain forensic analysis of the forged bridge message"). The facts landed publicly in **May 2026** — LayerZero's two-part incident report, Chainalysis's and Blockaid's analyses — and the file was never revisited. **A sourceless placeholder aged into a cited classification**, which is precisely the pattern the 2026-07 fixture-provenance sweep was run to catch, here occurring in a release file rather than a fixture.

Four specific facts were corrected against the sources now cited:

1. **No signing key was compromised.** The DVN's attestation was valid and produced by the legitimate key. T10.001 is therefore excluded, not merely de-prioritised.
2. **The attribution is confirmed, not pseudonymous.** TraderTraitor / UNC4899, per Mandiant, CrowdStrike and zeroShadow, named in LayerZero's own report. The corpus's largest 2026 bridge incident was sitting unlinked to [`OAK-G01`](../actors/OAK-G01-lazarus.md).
3. **The source chain was Unichain**, not Arbitrum as the previous text implied in its bridging example.
4. **The Aave cascade mechanism was wrong.** The previous text described pre-existing rsETH-collateralised loans falling underwater. What actually happened is that the **attacker deposited the unbacked rsETH into Aave V3 and borrowed against it** — an active step, not a passive repricing. Several unsourced figures from the prior version (a flat "~\$200M in rsETH-collateralised loans outstanding", named DEX venues, a treasury-capacity claim) have been replaced with sourced ones or dropped.

### Why the class matters more than the correction

With the mapping fixed, this becomes the **largest anchor for `OAK-T10.002.001`** by two orders of magnitude, and it establishes a **third sub-shape** alongside parse-level (Across) and semantic (Coreum, Chainflip): **(c) infrastructure-level source forgery**, in which the observer's *data source* is subverted so that it parses correctly and reasons correctly over events that never occurred. The distinction is operationally sharp. Against (a) the fix is a type check on the parsed record; against (b) it is a custody reconciliation; against (c) **neither the parser nor the message is defective** — the fix is **source diversity with integrity attestation, quorum across independently operated observers, and fail-closed behaviour when the source set shrinks.**

The sub-shape has a second anchor at the opposite end of the loss scale: **Long** (2026-09-14, 46.7928 WETH / ~\$118K), where a **third-party RPC fed a custodial bridge's keeper fabricated withdrawal events** and the keeper released from its Robinhood Chain vault. Same shape, one four-hundredth the size, no state actor, no six-week operation — which is the useful part. **The technique does not require DPRK-grade capability**; at the small end it requires only that a keeper trust one RPC provider.

The corpus-level reading is the uncomfortable one. OAK carried this incident for five months under a mapping that pointed defenders at **key management** — the one control surface that was never in play. A framework's cost of a wrong mapping is not the wrong label; it is **every reader who checked their HSM policy and considered the lesson applied.**
